## 1. Case summary (facts only)

On the night of 25–26 October 2014, Chelsea Bruck, 22, of Maybee, Michigan,
attended a large outdoor Halloween costume party on Post Road in Frenchtown
Township, Monroe County. Reported attendance was in the hundreds — more than
600 by one account — with multiple bands. She wore a costume she had made
herself: Poison Ivy, the Batman character. She had left her phone in the
vehicle that drove her there, and became separated from her ride home. She was
last seen in the early hours of 26 October walking toward the parking area with
a man nobody could identify. [10-06-S01, S02, S03, S05]

She was not found for six months. On 5 April 2015 parts of her costume and
clothing were recovered from an abandoned industrial site in Flat Rock, Wayne
County — about 15 miles from the party. On 24 April 2015, workers trying to free
a stuck dump truck at a home construction site on a privately owned wooded lot
off Briar Hill Road in Ash Township, Monroe County, uncovered human remains.
The Wayne County Medical Examiner's Office confirmed by dental records the
following afternoon that they were Chelsea Bruck's. Cause of death: blunt force
trauma to the head. [S06, S07, S08, S09]

The case broke on the costume. Male DNA recovered from the costume was matched
in CODIS in July 2016 to Daniel Clay of Newport — a match made possible by a
2015 Michigan law permitting DNA collection from people on arrest. Clay was
taken into custody on 22 July 2016, told investigators Bruck had died during
consensual erotic asphyxiation and that he had panicked and hidden her body,
rejected a plea offer, testified in his own defence, and on 16 May 2017 was
convicted of first-degree felony murder and concealing the death of an
individual. He was sentenced on 13 July 2017 to life without parole. The
Michigan Court of Appeals affirmed on 8 January 2019, and the Michigan Supreme
Court denied leave on 3 March 2020. [S10, S11, S12, S13, S14, S15, S16]

## 6. Halloween connection

**Classification: B — the disappearance occurred during documented Halloween
activity.** Not A. Not C.

- The party was a **Halloween costume party**, held the weekend before
  Halloween: it began on the evening of **25 October 2014** and she vanished
  in the early hours of **26 October 2014**.
- She wore a **costume she had made herself** — Poison Ivy.
- **That costume became the central piece of physical evidence in the case.**
  It was recovered separately from her body, six months later and 15 miles
  away, and the male DNA on it is what identified the man convicted of killing
  her.

This is the rare case where the Halloween element is not atmosphere: the
Halloween costume is the evidentiary spine of the prosecution. That is the
honest and much stronger connection, and it requires no date inflation.

**The murder did not occur on 31 October.** The party was not a Halloween-night
party. Do not imply either.

### Correct legal labels
- Clay **may** be described as: convicted of first-degree felony murder;
  convicted of concealing her death; serving life without parole; "the man
  convicted of killing Chelsea Bruck"; and — since the conviction is final —
  as her killer.
- Note the precise conviction: **first-degree felony murder**, not premeditated
  murder. Michigan felony murder is murder committed in the course of an
  enumerated felony (here, the underlying sexual assault). Some reporting says
  "first-degree murder" without qualification. Prefer "first-degree felony
  murder".
- Do **not** say he "confessed to murder." He admitted causing her death and
  concealing her body, but maintained it was an accident. The jury rejected
  that; his account is not a murder confession.

## 8. Conflicts and uncertainties

**1. Last-seen time.** Reported as "about 3 a.m.", "3:15 a.m.", "between 2 and
3 a.m." and "between 3 and 4 a.m." across CBS, ABC, NBC and clickondetroit.
→ Strongest sources are the contemporaneous sheriff's appeals, which say "about
3 a.m." → **Script wording:** "sometime around three in the morning."
**Do not state 3:15 a.m. as fact.**

**2. Last-seen date: 25 vs 26 October.** The Michigan Court of Appeals says she
was "last seen alive on October 25, 2014"; news coverage overwhelmingly says
the early hours of 26 October.
→ Both are reconcilable: the party began on the 25th, she vanished after
midnight on the 26th. → **Script wording:** "She went to the party on the
night of October 25th. By three in the morning on the 26th, she was gone."

**3. Clay's age at arrest: 27 or 28.** clickondetroit and WXYZ headlines say
27; CBS/Fox say 28.
→ Unresolved. → **Script wording:** omit his age, or say "then in his
twenties."

**4. Party attendance: 600, 600–1,000, or "hundreds".** Figures vary.
→ **Script wording:** "a crowd of several hundred" or "more than six hundred
people," attributed. Do not say 1,000 flatly.

**5. Flat Rock road name: "Peters Road" vs "Peers Road(s)".** Both spellings
appear; "Peters Road near Van Horn Road" is the more consistent rendering.
→ **Script wording:** "an abandoned industrial site in Flat Rock." Avoid the
street name unless verified.

**6. Costume/wig description.** "Green leotard with fake leaves and a red wig"
(Detroit News) vs "a top made of ivy leaves and a black wig that appeared red
at the ends" (ABC).
→ **Script wording:** "a Poison Ivy costume she had made herself — leaves, and
a wig." Do not over-specify.

**7. Mother's name: Leannda vs Leann.** Reporting gives "Leannda Bruck."
→ Use **Leannda Bruck**, or "Chelsea's mother."

**8. The separate assault sentence: 40–75 or 45–75 years.** The Toledo Blade
headline says 45 to 75; clickondetroit's summary says 40 to 75.
→ **Script wording:** "decades more in the separate case." **VERIFY BEFORE
BROADCAST** if the figure is used.

**9. Michigan Supreme Court docket number.** Reported as 159138 in one
secondary summary; the number was not independently confirmed.
→ Cite the **date** (3 March 2020) and the outcome (leave denied), not the
docket number.

**10. "Confessed in April 2016."** Several outlets — including wire-derived
copy — say Clay "admitted killing Bruck during a police interrogation in April
2016." This conflicts with the documented sequence: the CODIS match came 21
July 2016 and he was taken into custody 22 July 2016.
→ Strongest sources: the arrest-date reporting and the appellate opinion's
sequence (DNA match → identification as suspect → questioning).
→ **Do not use "April 2016."** Say "after his arrest in July 2016."

**11. Initial charge: second-degree murder vs "open murder".** Both appear.
→ **Script wording:** "He was first charged with second-degree murder" — then
note he was ultimately convicted of first-degree felony murder. This escalation
is itself a legitimate story beat.

## 9. Story engine

### Hook candidates (all factually supported)
1. She made the costume herself. It is the reason anyone knows who killed her.
   [S06, S10, S12]
2. Her body was found in April. Her costume was found nineteen days earlier,
   fifteen miles away. [S06, S07]
3. She left her phone in the car. That one detail removed every ordinary way
   out of that night. [S12]
4. Six hundred people at the party. Photographs from dozens of phones. And for
   eighteen months, nobody could say who the man walking beside her was.
   [S03, S05]
5. A sketch of a man in a plain black hooded sweatshirt and black-framed
   glasses was all the investigation had — and it never identified him.
   [S02, S03]
6. What identified him was a Michigan law that took effect in 2015, after she
   disappeared. [S12]
7. Workers freeing a stuck dump truck at a construction site found her.
   [S07, S08]
8. He didn't deny causing her death. He told a jury it was an accident. The
   medical examiner had counted the fractures in her face and jaw. [S12, S20]
9. He turned down thirty-five years, took the stand, and got life without
   parole. [S27, S13, S15]
10. Her memorial is a culinary scholarship — the school she had just started
    working toward. [S34, S09]

### Core mystery / question
For eighteen months: **who was the man in the photographs nobody could name?**
The party was crowded, photographed and public, and the one person who mattered
was invisible in it. After July 2016 the question becomes: **was it murder or
the accident he described?** — which the jury answered.

### Major revelation
**The costume.** Recovered separately from her body, 15 miles away at an
abandoned industrial site, carrying male DNA. The thing she made for a
Halloween party is what convicted her killer.

### Investigation turning point
**21 July 2016 — the CODIS hit.** Not the sketch, not the searches, not the
crowd-sourced photographs: a database match made possible by a Michigan law
that only took effect in 2015, after she died. Arrest followed the next day.

### Ending (strongest factual)
The legal record is closed: convicted 16 May 2017, life without parole on
13 July 2017, affirmed by the Court of Appeals on 8 January 2019, leave denied
by the Michigan Supreme Court on 3 March 2020. **This case is resolved** — say
so plainly. Optional close: the scholarship in the culinary programme she had
just begun to aim at.

## 12. Do not say

### Do not say
- ❌ "Halloween night" / "on Halloween" / "October 31, 2014"
- Why it is wrong: the party was on **25–26 October 2014**, the weekend before
  Halloween. The brief flags this explicitly.
- Correct wording: "a Halloween party the weekend before Halloween, on the
  night of October 25th, 2014."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Her body was found with her costume."
- Why it is wrong: the costume was recovered **19 days earlier and about 15
  miles away**, in a different county, at an abandoned industrial site. That
  separation is precisely what makes the costume important.
- Correct wording: "Her costume turned up first — in Flat Rock, fifteen miles
  away. Her body was found nineteen days later."

### Do not say
- ❌ "He confessed to murdering her."
- Why it is wrong/legally dangerous: he admitted causing her death and hiding
  her body but maintained it was an accident during consensual sex. That is
  not a murder confession. The **jury** rejected his account.
- Correct wording: "He admitted she died and that he hid her body — but told
  the jury it was an accident. The jury rejected that."

### Do not say
- ❌ "He was convicted of premeditated murder."
- Why it is wrong: the conviction was **first-degree felony murder** — murder
  in the course of another felony — plus concealing a death.
- Correct wording: "first-degree felony murder and concealing the death of an
  individual."

### Do not say
- ❌ "He confessed in April 2016."
- Why it is wrong: widely repeated but inconsistent with the documented
  sequence. The DNA match was 21 July 2016; he was in custody on 22 July 2016.
- Correct wording: "After his arrest in July 2016, he told investigators…"

### Do not say
- ❌ "The sketch caught him." / "A witness identified him."
- Why it is wrong: the composite sketch circulated for roughly 18 months
  without identifying anyone. The case was broken by a **CODIS DNA match**.
- Correct wording: "The sketch never found him. A database did."

### Do not say
- ❌ "She was last seen at 3:15 a.m."
- Why it is misleading: sources give at least four different times. Do not
  convert an approximate window into a precise timestamp.
- Correct wording: "around three in the morning."

### Do not say
- ❌ "*Dateline* returned to the case in 2024 with 'After the Halloween Party'."
- Why it is wrong: **"After the Halloween Party" (2024) is about the murder of
  Karen Swift in Dyersburg, Tennessee — a completely different case.** The
  similar title is a trap. The Bruck episode is "The Halloween Party" (2017).
- Correct wording: "*Dateline* covered the case in October 2017, in an episode
  called 'The Halloween Party'."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Police did nothing for six months" / "the investigation went cold."
- Why it is misleading: the sheriff's office ran public photo appeals,
  identified and interviewed people in party images, released a composite
  sketch, and hundreds of volunteers searched. It was unsolved, not dormant.
- Correct wording: "For eighteen months investigators had a sketch, a crowd of
  witnesses, and no name."

### Do not say
- ❌ Naming the party host, the property owners, the friend she rode with, or
  the witness.
- Why: none of those identities was verified here, and all are private
  individuals peripheral to the case.
- Correct wording: "the property where the party was held," "the friend she
  came with," "a witness."

### Do not say
- ❌ Graphic detail of the sexual assault or of the condition of her remains.
- Why: available in the record but gratuitous, and against the brief. The
  medical examiner's cause of death carries the necessary weight.
- Correct wording: "The medical examiner found she had died of blunt force
  trauma to the head."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Daniel Clay is appealing" / "his appeals are ongoing."
- Why it is wrong: direct appeals are exhausted — affirmed January 2019, leave
  denied March 2020. No pending proceeding was located.
- Correct wording: "His conviction was upheld on appeal in 2019, and the
  Michigan Supreme Court refused to hear the case in 2020."

## 14. Three-minute production assessment

- **Narrative complexity:** Medium. A clean four-beat structure — vanished from
  a crowded party / costume found / body found / DNA names him — but it spans
  2014 to 2020 and needs three separate geographic points.
- **Factual confidence:** High. A published appellate opinion, a documented
  trial, a named medical examiner finding, a named prosecutor and judge, and
  dense contemporaneous local and national reporting.
- **Legal complexity:** Medium. The conviction is final and he can properly be
  called her killer. The traps are narrower: felony murder vs premeditated
  murder, and the difference between admitting a death and confessing to murder.
- **Halloween connection strength:** **High** — the strongest in this batch,
  because the Halloween costume is the physical evidence that solved the case,
  not just the reason she was out that night. It needs no exaggeration, only
  accuracy about the date.
- **Strongest factual hook:** She made the costume herself, and the costume is
  what identified her killer.
- **Strongest evidence / revelation:** Male DNA on the recovered costume,
  matched in CODIS in July 2016 — a match only possible because of a Michigan
  law that took effect in 2015, after she was already dead.
- **Strongest ending:** Conviction, mandatory life without parole, affirmed in
  2019, leave denied in 2020 — closed. Optional coda: the culinary scholarship.
- **Biggest production risk:** Collapsing the two discovery dates into one
  "found with her costume" beat, which destroys the case's actual logic and is
  factually wrong.
- **Biggest factual risk:** The *Dateline* title trap ("After the Halloween
  Party," 2024, is a different victim), and the "confessed in April 2016"
  error that is embedded in wire-derived copy.
- **Recommended visual themes:** A field party at night — string lights, a
  stage, a crowd of costumes; a single phone left on a car seat; ivy leaves and
  a green fabric panel; a derelict industrial lot with chain-link and cracked
  concrete; a wooded lot with heavy equipment; a three-point map of Frenchtown
  Township → Ash Township → Flat Rock; a CODIS-style database match rendered
  abstractly; a courtroom bench. Avoid any depiction of the assault, the
  remains, or the actual costume as evidence.
