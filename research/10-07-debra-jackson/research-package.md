## 1. Case summary (facts only)

On 31 October 1979 the nude body of a young woman was found face-down in a
concrete culvert beside Interstate 35, north of Georgetown in Williamson County,
Texas. She was wearing nothing but a pair of orange socks, and had a
pearl-stone ring. She had been sexually assaulted; the medical examiner
determined she had been strangled and ruled the death a homicide. Nobody could
identify her. Investigators estimated she was between 15 and 30 years old,
5 ft 8 in to 5 ft 10 in, 140–160 lb, with long brown hair and teeth in very good
condition with no dental work. When no one claimed her, she was buried in Odd
Fellows Cemetery in Georgetown under a marker that read only that she was an
unidentified woman. She became known as "Orange Socks."
[10-07-S01, S02, S03, S04]

In 1982, Henry Lee Lucas confessed to killing her. In 1984 a Williamson County
jury convicted him of capital murder and he was sentenced to death — the only
death sentence among his murder convictions. The only evidence linking him to
the killing was his own confession. He recanted. In April 1986 Texas Attorney
General Jim Mattox published a report finding that Lucas's mass confessions
were largely a hoax, that there was a notable lack of physical evidence, and
that there were numerous discrepancies between his confessions and evidence of
his whereabouts — including roofing-company records in Jacksonville, Florida
indicating he was working there at the relevant time. In May 1998 the Texas
Board of Pardons and Paroles recommended commutation, and on 26 June 1998
Governor George W. Bush commuted Lucas's death sentence to life, roughly 48
hours before he was to be executed. Lucas died in prison in March 2001.
[S05, S06, S07, S08, S09, S10]

The identification came four decades later. The Williamson County Sheriff's
Office cold case unit released a new forensic sketch in 2016, sent 1979 autopsy
evidence for modern DNA testing in 2018, and worked with the DNA Doe Project.
In August 2019, after the updated portrait was released to the press again, a
woman who had grown up in Abilene and had recently moved to Georgetown
recognised her own missing sister. DNA comparison confirmed it. On 7 August
2019, Sheriff Robert Chody announced that "Orange Socks" was Debra Louise
Jackson, 23, of Abilene, born 20 September 1956, who had left her family's home
in 1977 and never returned. Her family had never reported her missing, because
she had gone away before. [S02, S03, S11, S12, S13, S14]

**Identifying her did not solve her murder. It has not been solved.**
[S15, S16]

## 6. Halloween connection

**Classification: C — the central discovery of the body occurred on 31 October.**
It is **not** A (the crime itself is not established as having occurred on the
31st) and **not** B (no Halloween activity is involved).

Precise, defensible statement:

- Her body was found on **Halloween morning, 31 October 1979**. [S01, S02, S03]
- Her date of death is recorded as **30 or 31 October 1979** — so the killing
  may have occurred the day before. The discovery date is the fixed point.
  [S12, S17]
- Contemporary and current reporting consistently frames her as "the woman
  found dead on Halloween" — including KXAN's October 2025 piece, whose own
  framing is "46 years after woman found dead on Halloween." [S15]
- **Nothing about Halloween is connected to the crime itself.** She was not at
  a Halloween event, was not in costume, and the date appears to be
  coincidence.

This is a clean, honest type-C connection and needs no embellishment: an
unidentified woman found beside a Texas interstate on Halloween morning, who
stayed nameless for nearly forty years. The date is the anniversary the
sheriff's office and local press themselves use.

Use language of this shape, and do not compress it:

> "Henry Lee Lucas confessed to killing her in 1982, and in 1984 a Williamson
> County jury convicted him of capital murder on the strength of that confession
> alone. He recanted. Two years later the Texas Attorney General reported that
> Lucas's confessions were largely a hoax, and that records placed him in

## 8. Conflicts and uncertainties

**1. Date of death: 30 or 31 October 1979.**
Grave and reference records give "October 30 or 31, 1979"; the discovery is
firmly 31 October.
→ **Script wording:** "Her body was found on the morning of October 31st, 1979."
State the discovery date, not a date of death.

**2. Surname: Jackson or Larned.**
The sheriff's announcement and all news coverage use **Debra Louise Jackson**.
The cemetery/Find a Grave record is under **Debra Louise "Debra Jackson"
Larned**, and one 2025 report notes she was "also known as Debra Louise
Larned."
→ **Script wording:** "Debra Louise Jackson — recorded in some places under the
name Larned." Do not silently pick one and ignore the other.

**3. Time of discovery, and who found her.**
"Approximately 4:30 a.m., a highway maintenance worker" comes from a secondary
account; other retellings vary.
→ **Script wording:** "early on Halloween morning." **Do not state 4:30 a.m.**
and do not assert who found her unless verified against the 1979 record.

**4. Mile marker 268.**
Appears in secondary sources only.
→ **Script wording:** "in a culvert beside Interstate 35, just north of
Georgetown." Omit the mile marker unless verified.

**5. Lucas's date of death: 12 or 13 March 2001.**
UPI's archive is dated 13 March 2001; other sources give 12 March 2001 at
11 p.m. (which may be the same event reported across a date boundary).
→ **Script wording:** "in March 2001."

**6. Lucas's confession count and conviction count.**
"Confessed to over 200 murders," "hundreds," and "confessed to some 600" all
circulate; conviction counts appear as 11 murder convictions, and separately as
"serving hundreds of years for nine murder convictions in Texas."
→ **Script wording:** "He confessed to hundreds of murders" and "he was
convicted of eleven." Both are supportable; avoid a precise confession figure.

**7. Year of the confession: 1982 vs 1983.**
Most sources say he confessed to this killing in 1982; the Netflix series
frames the wider Lucas story around 1983.
→ **Script wording:** "In 1982" (the more consistently reported year for this
case), or "in the early 1980s."

**8. Whether Bush's commutation was his only one as governor.**
Widely reported as the only commutation George W. Bush granted as Governor of
Texas, but not confirmed by a source located in this session.
→ **Do not state it** unless verified. The safe, sourced line is simply that he
commuted this sentence citing doubts about guilt.

**9. Whether the 2019 identification was genetic genealogy or a recognition.**
Some coverage frames it as a DNA Doe Project genealogy success; other coverage
(including the Austin American-Statesman and KVUE) makes clear the immediate
trigger was **the sister recognising the 2016 forensic sketch on television**,
with DNA then confirming it.
→ Strongest sources support **both, operating together**: DDP's genealogy work
had reached a potential cousin, and the sketch prompted the sister's call; the
sister's DNA then confirmed the match via GEDmatch.
→ **Script wording:** "Genetic genealogy had narrowed it to a possible cousin.
What actually named her was her own sister, watching the news, recognising a
sketch. Then the DNA confirmed it." This is the truthful and the better version.

**10. Age estimate vs actual age.**
The 1979 estimate was 15–30; she was 23.
→ Usable as a factual detail about the limits of 1979 forensics.

## 9. Story engine

### Hook candidates (all factually supported)
1. On Halloween morning in 1979, a woman was found beside Interstate 35 wearing
   nothing but a pair of orange socks. For forty years that was her name.
   [S01, S02]
2. Her teeth were perfect. No fillings, no dental work — which is exactly why
   dental records could not identify her. [S01]
3. Nobody reported her missing. She had left home before, so her family
   waited. [S12, S16]
4. A man confessed, was convicted, and was sentenced to die for killing her.
   His own state's Attorney General later reported he was in Florida at the
   time. [S05, S07]
5. Forty-eight hours before his execution, the Governor of Texas stopped it —
   because there were too many doubts that he had done it. [S08, S09]
6. His conviction was never overturned. And the sheriff's office is still
   looking for her killer. [S15, §7]
7. What finally named her was not a database. It was her sister, watching the
   news, recognising a police sketch. [S13, S21]
8. The evidence that cracked it had been sitting in storage since 1979 —
   fingernail clippings taken at her autopsy. [S11, S23]
9. DNA from those 1979 samples came back with more than one male contributor.
   None of them has been named. [S23]
10. Her headstone said "unidentified woman" for forty years. Someone donated the
    one with her name on it. [S18]
11. Forty-six years on, the question the sheriff's office is still asking is not
    who she was. It is where she was between 1977 and 1979. [S16]

### Core mystery / question
The case has **two** questions, and the second outlasts the first.
First: *who was she?* — answered on 7 August 2019.
Second: *who killed her?* — **unanswered**, and the answer that was recorded for
thirty-five years appears to have been wrong.

### Major revelation
Not the identification. It is that **the case was "closed" by a confession that
was worthless** — the man convicted and condemned for her murder was, per the
Texas Attorney General's own 1986 report, confessing to killings he could not
have committed, with payroll records placing him in another state. The system
had an answer for decades. It was the wrong shape of answer.

### Investigation turning point
Two, in sequence:
- **2018** — 1979 autopsy evidence, still preserved, sent for modern DNA
  testing, and the DNA Doe Project taking the case.
- **August 2019** — the re-release of the 2016 forensic sketch, and the sister
  who saw it. The sketch, not the database, is what put a name on her.

### Ending (strongest factual)
**Explicitly unresolved.** She has her name back and a headstone that carries
it. Her murder is an open investigation with the Williamson County Sheriff's
Office. DNA from more than one man was recovered from evidence kept since 1979,
and nobody has been identified. The only person ever convicted of killing her
was convicted on a confession that his own state concluded was unreliable, and
he died in prison in 2001 with the question still open. As of October 2025,
detectives are still asking the public where Debra Jackson was between 1977 and
1979.

**Never imply resolution.**

## 12. Do not say

### Do not say
- ❌ "Henry Lee Lucas killed her" / "her killer, Henry Lee Lucas" / "the man who
  murdered her"
- Why it is wrong: his conviction rested on a confession he recanted, which the
  Texas Attorney General's 1986 report found unreliable, with records placing
  him in another state. The Governor of Texas commuted his death sentence
  citing doubts about his guilt. The sheriff's office investigates the murder as
  unsolved.
- Correct wording: "Henry Lee Lucas was convicted of her murder in 1984 on the
  strength of a confession he later took back — a confession Texas's own
  Attorney General concluded was unreliable. Nobody has been established as her
  killer."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Lucas's conviction was overturned" / "thrown out" / "vacated" / "he was
  exonerated"
- Why it is wrong: none of that happened. His **death sentence** was commuted to
  life in 1998. The conviction itself stood until he died and stands today.
- Correct wording: "His death sentence was commuted to life. The conviction
  itself was never overturned."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Identifying her solved the case."
- Why it is wrong: the brief flags this explicitly, and it is false. The
  identification answered who she was, not who killed her.
- Correct wording: "After forty years, they knew her name. They still do not
  know who killed her."

### Do not say
- ❌ "She was murdered on Halloween."
- Why it is misleading: her body was **found** on 31 October 1979; her date of
  death is recorded as 30 or 31 October. The Halloween connection is the
  discovery, not the crime.
- Correct wording: "Her body was found on Halloween morning, 1979."

### Do not say
- ❌ "She was killed at a Halloween party" / anything linking Halloween to the
  circumstances of the crime.
- Why it is wrong: there is no Halloween element in the crime at all. The date
  appears to be coincidence.
- Correct wording: keep Halloween strictly to the discovery date.

### Do not say
- ❌ "Genetic genealogy identified her" (as the whole story)
- Why it is incomplete: investigative genetic genealogy had narrowed the search
  to a potential cousin, but the identification was triggered by her sister
  recognising a 2016 forensic sketch on television, and confirmed by the
  sister's DNA.
- Correct wording: "Genealogy got them close. Her sister got them there."

### Do not say
- ❌ "DNA has identified her killer" / "there's a DNA profile of the man who did
  it."
- Why it is wrong and legally dangerous: DNA from **two or more male
  contributors** was recovered from 1979 evidence. It has not been publicly
  matched to anyone, and multiple contributors do not establish who any of them
  is or what any contribution means.
- Correct wording: "Testing on evidence kept since 1979 found DNA from more
  than one man. None of it has been matched to anyone."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Her family abandoned her" / "nobody cared she was gone."
- Why it is wrong and cruel: her family did not report her missing because she
  had left home before and they had reason to expect she might return. They
  supplied the photographs and the DNA that named her.
- Correct wording: "She had left home before, so when she left in 1977 her
  family waited. No missing-person report was ever filed."

### Do not say
- ❌ "The state of Texas admitted it convicted the wrong man."
- Why it is wrong: no such formal admission exists. The Attorney General's
  report found the confessions unreliable; the Board of Pardons and Paroles and
  the Governor cited **doubts**. That is not a finding of innocence.
- Correct wording: "Texas's own Attorney General reported the confessions were
  largely a hoax, and the Governor commuted the sentence citing doubts about
  guilt."

### Do not say
- ❌ "It was Bush's only act of clemency as governor."
- Why: plausible and widely repeated, but not verified here.
- Correct wording: omit, or attribute it explicitly once verified.

### Do not say
- ❌ "She was a hitchhiker" / "she was a runaway" / "she was working as a
  prostitute" or any similar characterisation.
- Why it is wrong: none of it is established. Lucas's discredited confession
  claimed he picked her up in Oklahoma; that is a discredited confession, not a
  fact about her life. What is documented is that she left home in 1977 and was
  working at a hotel in Amarillo in 1978.
- Correct wording: state only the documented facts about her life.

### Do not say
- ❌ Naming her sister or any living relative.
- Why: private individuals, and the sister's role is fully conveyable without a
  name.
- Correct wording: "her sister."

### Do not say
- ❌ Graphic description of the body or the assault.
- Why: gratuitous, and against the brief. Strangulation as cause of death and
  the fact of a sexual assault carry the necessary weight.

## 13. Unresolved questions

- **Who killed her.** Entirely open.
- Whose DNA the two-or-more male profiles from the 1979 evidence belong to.
- Her exact date and place of death, and where she was killed before being left
  in the culvert.
- Where she was and what she was doing between leaving Abilene in 1977 and her
  death in 1979, apart from the 1978 Amarillo job — the sheriff's office's own
  stated open question as of October 2025.
- Why she was on the I-35 corridor at all.
- Whether the Lucas conviction will ever be formally addressed by any court.
- Whether the surname Larned reflects a marriage, a family name, or a records
  variance.

## 14. Three-minute production assessment

- **Narrative complexity:** **High** — the hardest of the three. It has to carry
  a 1979 discovery, a forty-year anonymity, a 1984 conviction that was
  simultaneously real and worthless, a 1998 commutation, a 2019 identification,
  and a still-open murder. The Lucas material alone could swallow the runtime;
  he must be a chapter, not the subject.
- **Factual confidence:** **Medium-High.** The core — discovery, condition,
  cause of death, identification, unsolved status — is very well sourced,
  including a named sheriff's announcement, the Austin American-Statesman, and
  October 2025 follow-up. Several 1979 particulars (exact time, who found her,
  mile marker) rest on secondary accounts and are flagged.
- **Legal complexity:** **Very High** — the highest in this batch. A convicted
  but discredited confessor, a commuted sentence, an intact conviction, and a
  live unsolved investigation. Every sentence about Lucas needs care.
- **Halloween connection strength:** **High and clean** as a type-C connection.
  The body was found on Halloween morning; the sheriff's office and local press
  use that anniversary themselves. Nothing needs stretching.
- **Strongest factual hook:** For forty years the state's record of her was a
  pair of orange socks and a headstone that said "unidentified woman."
- **Strongest evidence / revelation:** That the man convicted and sentenced to
  death for her murder was, per Texas's own Attorney General, confessing to
  killings he could not have committed — with payroll records putting him in
  Florida. The case had an answer for thirty-five years, and it was the wrong
  one.
- **Strongest ending:** She has her name and a headstone that carries it. Her
  murder is open. DNA from more than one man, recovered from evidence kept since
  1979, has never been matched. Forty-six years on, detectives are still asking
  where she was between 1977 and 1979. **Explicitly unresolved.**
- **Biggest production risk:** Henry Lee Lucas hijacking the episode. He is the
  famous name and the search-traffic magnet, and centring him both distorts the
  case and makes it a story about a serial killer instead of about Debra
  Jackson.
- **Biggest factual risk:** Any phrasing that makes Lucas her killer, or that
  treats the 2019 identification as having solved the murder. Both are
  explicitly forbidden by the brief and both are easy to slip into.
- **Recommended visual themes:** A Texas interstate at dawn; a concrete culvert
  and highway right-of-way; a single pair of orange socks as the case's emblem;
  a blank grave marker versus a named one; a forensic sketch; a 1979 evidence
  envelope; a television screen in a living room (the sketch, the sister); a
  West Texas to Central Texas map (Abilene → Amarillo → Georgetown); Texas
  clemency paperwork. Keep Lucas visually minimal and clearly framed as the
  discredited chapter, not the answer. **Use no AI-generated "reconstruction"
  of her face** — the real forensic sketches are the historical artefacts and
  the AI substitute would be both false and, in this case, an insult to the
  thing that actually identified her.
