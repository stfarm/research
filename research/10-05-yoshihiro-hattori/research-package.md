## 1. Case summary (facts only)

On the evening of 17 October 1992, Yoshihiro Hattori, a 16-year-old Japanese
exchange student living in Baton Rouge, and his American host brother Webb
Haymaker went looking for a Halloween party organised for Japanese exchange
students. They went to the wrong house — a street address one digit-group
different from their destination. The homeowner's wife, Bonnie Peairs, opened a
door, was frightened, slammed and locked it, and told her husband to get his
gun. Rodney Peairs retrieved a loaded .44 Magnum revolver, opened the carport
door, shouted "Freeze," and fired once at close range, striking Hattori in the
chest. Hattori died. [10-05-S01, 10-05-S03, 10-05-S06, 10-05-S10]

No arrest was made that night. After diplomatic protest, the East Baton Rouge
Parish District Attorney convened a grand jury, which indicted Peairs for
manslaughter. A jury acquitted him in May 1993 after roughly three hours of
deliberation. Hattori's parents then won a civil wrongful-death and survival
judgment against Peairs in September 1994; the Louisiana First Circuit affirmed
it in 1995 and the Louisiana Supreme Court denied writs in 1996.
[10-05-S04, 10-05-S05, 10-05-S06, 10-05-S07, 10-05-S08]

**The two outcomes are the core of this case and must never be blurred:
acquitted of the crime, held liable for the death.**

## 6. Halloween connection

**Classification: B — the death occurred during documented Halloween activity.**
It is **not** classification A and **not** classification C.

Precise, defensible statement of the connection:

- The shooting occurred on **17 October 1992**, two weeks before Halloween.
- Hattori was **wearing a Halloween costume** (a *Saturday Night Fever* outfit
  he had rented) and was **carrying a camera**. [S09]
- He and Webb Haymaker were **actively searching for a Halloween party** that
  had been organised for Japanese exchange students. [S01]
- They mistook the Peairs house for the party house in part because **it was
  decorated for Halloween**. [S03]
- The word "Halloween" appears in the Louisiana Court of Appeal's own
  recitation of the facts. [S07]

So the Halloween element is not decoration added by later retellings — it is in
the court record, it explains the costume, and it explains the mistake. But the
date is 17 October. Any line that implies 31 October is false.

### Correct legal labels
- Peairs may be described as: **the man who shot and killed Yoshihiro Hattori**;
  **acquitted of manslaughter**; **found liable in civil court for Hattori's
  wrongful death**; **found by a civil court to have committed an intentional
  tort**.
- Peairs may **not** be described as: a convicted killer, a murderer, guilty of
  manslaughter, or a criminal. He is none of those things in law.

## 8. Conflicts and uncertainties

**1. Died at the scene vs. died en route to hospital.**
Some accounts say emergency responders arrived and Hattori died on the way to
hospital; others simply say he was killed. Strongest source (appellate
recitation, S07) establishes the fatal chest wound but the extract does not
settle place of death.
→ **Script wording:** "He was shot once in the chest and died."

**2. Date given as 18 October 1992 in one outlet.**
The Garden Island (S23) states 18 October. Every court record, contemporary
wire report and archival source gives **17 October 1992**.
→ Strongest source: the Louisiana First Circuit opinion (S07). Use 17 October.

**3. Petition signature totals.**
Carleton College's long-form feature (S02) gives ~1.7 million Japanese
signatures plus ~150,000 US signatures. Other retellings give a combined
figure of about 1.65 million.
→ **Script wording:** "more than 1.5 million signatures in Japan and roughly
150,000 in the United States" — or avoid the number and say "well over a
million signatures". Do not present a single precise total as settled.

**4. Civil award: $650,000 vs $653,077.85.**
Wire coverage (S05) rounds to $650,000. The appellate opinion (S07) gives the
exact figure $653,077.85.
→ Strongest source: the opinion. Use **$653,077.85**, or "about $653,000".

**5. Peairs's age: 30 or 31.**
He was 30 at the shooting (Oct 1992) and 31 at trial (May 1993). Both figures
appear and both are correct in context. [S01, S04]
→ **Script wording:** "the 30-year-old homeowner" for October 1992.

**6. Webb Haymaker's date of death: 1 or 2 March 2022.**
The obituary record indicates 1 March 2022; The Trace's feature says 2 March.
→ **Script wording:** "in March 2022" unless the obituary is confirmed directly.

**7. Louisiana "shoot the burglar" law.**
A widely repeated claim holds that Louisiana's permissive
defence-of-habitation rule dates to 1976, and a separate claim holds that
Louisiana broadened its self-defence law *because of* this case. Neither is
established by a source strong enough to broadcast; the sources found are a
CourseHero snippet and general commentary.
→ **Do not state a statute year or a causal legislative link.** If legal
context is needed, attribute it: "Louisiana law at the time gave homeowners
broad latitude to use deadly force against a perceived intruder — latitude the
civil court found Peairs had exceeded." [supported by S07]

**8. Whether the case "caused" the Brady Act.**
The Brady Act was enacted 30 November 1993, in the same period as the
petition campaign. Causation is not established.
→ **Script wording:** "Their petitions reached the White House weeks before
the Brady Bill became law" — chronology, not causation.

## 9. Sensitive-handling note (mandatory read)

The Trace reported in November 2022 that **Webb Haymaker died by suicide in
March 2022, aged 46**, and that he had been affected for decades by having
witnessed the shooting. [S11, S21]

Guidance for the production agent:
- This is reputably sourced and is arguably the most devastating factual
  element in the case's aftermath. It is also a suicide involving a
  identifiable private individual and his surviving family.
- If included, include **no method, no detail, and no speculation about
  cause**. State only what the reporting supports: that he died in March 2022
  and that his family said he had long been affected by what he witnessed.
- A three-minute runtime is a poor vehicle for it. **Recommended default:
  omit**, and end on the legal and civic outcome instead (§10 "Ending").
- If the channel does include it, follow standard safe-messaging practice for
  the platform.

## 10. Story engine

### Hook candidates (all factually supported)
1. Two teenagers in Halloween costumes rang a doorbell on the wrong street
   number — one digit-group off — and one of them did not survive it. [S03]
2. The house they picked was decorated for Halloween. That is why they thought
   they had the right one. [S03]
3. The last word Yoshi Hattori heard was "Freeze." He did not know what it
   meant in that context. [S03, S06]
4. He was carrying a camera. Not a weapon. The shooter admitted at trial he
   never saw a weapon. [S09]
5. A jury took about three hours to decide the man who shot him had committed
   no crime. [S04]
6. Sixteen months later, a judge looked at the same shooting and called it an
   intentional tort. [S07]
7. Acquitted of the killing; ordered to pay $653,077.85 for it. Same shooting,
   two courts, two answers. [S04, S07]
8. His mother's response was a petition. It ended up at the White House with
   over a million signatures behind it. [S02]
9. The shooting is in Louisiana's own appellate case law, and the word
   "Halloween" is in the court's statement of facts. [S07]
10. Thirty years later, his parents announced they were done campaigning —
    they said America had gone backward. [S11]

### Core mystery / question
Not "who did it" — that was never in doubt. The question is **how the same
act could be simultaneously no crime and a legal wrong**, and what that gap
says about the law that governed it.

### Major revelation
The **split verdicts**: a criminal jury acquitted in about three hours; a civil
judge, hearing the same facts, rejected self-defence outright, found an
intentional tort, refused to assign any fault to the two boys, and was
affirmed on appeal and left undisturbed by the state supreme court.

### Investigation turning point
There was almost no "investigation" in the ordinary sense — the shooter was
known immediately. The turning point is **prosecutorial**: the initial decision
not to charge, reversed after the Japanese consul general and the Governor of
Louisiana became involved, leading to the grand jury indictment of
4 November 1992. Without that, there is no case at all.

### Ending (strongest factual)
The legal record closed on 12 January 1996, when the Louisiana Supreme Court
declined to disturb the judgment — the acquittal and the liability both
standing, permanently, side by side. Optional civic coda: the petitions, and
the Hattoris' 2022 retirement from advocacy saying the country had moved
backward. **This case is legally resolved; it is not an unsolved mystery, and
should not be framed as one.**

## 13. Do not say

### Do not say
- ❌ "Halloween night, 1992" / "on Halloween" / "October 31"
- Why it is wrong: the shooting was **17 October 1992**. Several YouTube
  creators and at least one aggregator open with "Halloween night."
- Correct wording: "Two weeks before Halloween, on October 17, 1992, dressed
  for a Halloween party he never reached…"

### Do not say
- ❌ "Rodney Peairs was convicted" / "the killer was sentenced"
- Why it is wrong: he was **acquitted** of manslaughter and has no conviction.
- Correct wording: "A jury acquitted Rodney Peairs of manslaughter. A civil
  court later held him liable for Hattori's death."

### Do not say
- ❌ "murdered" / "murder of Yoshihiro Hattori"
- Why it is wrong/misleading: no murder charge was ever brought; the charge was
  manslaughter and it ended in acquittal. Calling it murder misstates the
  record and is legally dangerous.
- Correct wording: "the killing of Yoshihiro Hattori" or "the fatal shooting
  of Yoshihiro Hattori."

### Do not say
- ❌ "He got away with it" / "he walked free with no consequences"
- Why it is misleading: he was acquitted criminally but was held liable for
  $653,077.85 in a judgment affirmed on appeal and left standing by the state
  supreme court.
- Correct wording: "He faced no criminal punishment. He did face a judgment —
  $653,077.85, upheld on appeal."

### Do not say
- ❌ "The jury found it was self-defence."
- Why it is wrong: an acquittal means the State failed to prove manslaughter
  beyond reasonable doubt; it is not an affirmative finding of self-defence.
  The civil court expressly **rejected** self-defence.
- Correct wording: "The jury acquitted him. A judge, applying a different
  standard, rejected his self-defence claim outright."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Hattori was shot for knocking on a door."
- Why it is imprecise: the boys rang the bell, moved under the carport, and
  the shot came when Peairs opened the carport door and Hattori approached.
- Correct wording: "He rang the bell, then walked back toward the carport —
  and that is where he was shot."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Louisiana passed its 'shoot the burglar' law because of this case."
- Why it is wrong/unverified: no reliable source found supports a causal
  legislative link, and the statutory history claims in circulation trace to
  low-quality sources.
- Correct wording: "Louisiana law at the time gave homeowners broad latitude
  to use deadly force — latitude the civil court found Peairs had exceeded."

### Do not say
- ❌ "This case created the Brady Bill."
- Why it is wrong: the Brady Act had a long legislative history. The
  petitions coincided with, and added public pressure ahead of, its enactment.
- Correct wording: "Their petitions reached the White House weeks before the
  Brady Bill became law."

### Do not say
- ❌ Anything asserting Peairs's current address, current job, current
  finances, or that his assets are still being seized.
- Why it is wrong: those claims trace to a message-board post and a
  people-search aggregator. Unverified, privacy-invasive and legally risky.
- Correct wording: omit entirely.

### Do not say
- ❌ "It was a hate crime" / "he shot him because he was Japanese."
- Why it is wrong: no court made any such finding; no hate-crime charge was
  brought. The case *prompted* widespread discussion of American attitudes
  toward Asians, which is a different and verifiable statement.
- Correct wording: "The case set off an international argument about American
  gun culture and about how Americans see foreigners — but no court ever
  found a racial motive."

### Do not say
- ❌ "Hattori spoke no English" / any invented dialogue.
- Why it is wrong: the record supports that he continued forward, laughing,
  saying they were there for a party [S07]. Anything beyond that is invention.
- Correct wording: quote only what the opinion supports.

## 14. Unresolved questions

- The exact clock time of the shot is not established in accessible sources.
- Whether Hattori died at the scene or en route to hospital.
- The precise terms and history of collection on the civil judgment.
- Whether any Louisiana statutory change followed the case (claim circulates;
  unverified).
- Full contents of the LSU Haymaker Collection (Mss. 4698) — the single most
  promising unexploited primary source for this case.

## 15. Three-minute production assessment

- **Narrative complexity:** Medium. Two trials with opposite outcomes is the
  whole structure; it is simple to state but must be stated precisely.
- **Factual confidence:** High. This case has a published appellate opinion,
  a state supreme court writ denial, contemporaneous wire reporting, dated AP
  photographs and a university archival collection. It is the best-documented
  of the three cases in this batch.
- **Legal complexity:** High. The acquittal/liability split is the single
  easiest thing in this batch to get wrong, and getting it wrong is
  defamatory.
- **Halloween connection strength:** Medium–High as *documented Halloween
  activity* (costume, party, decorated house, the word "Halloween" in the
  court's own findings) — but **Low** as a calendar connection. The date is
  17 October. Package it as "the Halloween party he never reached," never as
  a Halloween-night killing.
- **Strongest factual hook:** The house numbers — 10131 and 10311 on the same
  street, and the wrong one was decorated for Halloween.
- **Strongest evidence / revelation:** The civil court's findings: deadly force
  not justified, conduct an intentional tort, **zero** fault assigned to the
  two boys — affirmed on appeal.
- **Strongest ending:** 12 January 1996 — the Louisiana Supreme Court declines
  to touch it, and the acquittal and the liability stand together for good.
- **Biggest production risk:** Halloween-night framing for thumbnail appeal.
  It is the one thing the brief explicitly forbids and the one thing every
  competing video does.
- **Biggest factual risk:** Calling Peairs a killer/murderer in the legal
  sense, or describing the acquittal as a finding of self-defence.
- **Recommended visual themes:** Suburban carport and porch light at dusk;
  two mailbox numbers side by side; a rented white tuxedo jacket; a 1990s
  compact camera; courthouse steps and press microphones; a stack of petition
  pages; wire-service typeface headlines; Nagoya cityscape for the family's
  side. Avoid any depiction of the shooting itself and avoid the current
  property.
